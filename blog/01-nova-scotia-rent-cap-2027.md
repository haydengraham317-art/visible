# Nova Scotia's Rent Cap in 2027: How Much You Can Raise Rent, and the Deadline Most Landlords Miss

If you own a rental property in Bedford, Halifax, or anywhere else in HRM and you are planning a
rent increase for January 1, your window is closing right now.

Nova Scotia requires **four months' written notice** before a rent increase takes effect. Work
backwards from January 1, 2027 and the notice needs to be in your tenant's hands by roughly the
start of September. Miss it, and the increase is not merely delayed — it is invalid, and you wait
another full year.

Here is what the cap allows, what it does not cover, and how to serve notice so it actually holds
up.

## How much can a landlord raise rent in Nova Scotia?

**A maximum of 5% in any 12-month period**, for the same tenant in the same unit.

Nova Scotia introduced the cap as a temporary measure and has extended it more than once. It is
currently legislated to run through **December 31, 2027**.

Two limits work together, and landlords routinely miss the second one:

1. **Amount:** no more than 5% above the current rent.
2. **Frequency:** no more than **once every 12 months**, counted from the last increase — not from
   the lease anniversary, and not from January 1.

If you raised rent on August 1, 2026, your next lawful increase is August 1, 2027 at the earliest,
regardless of when the lease term ends.

### What 5% actually looks like

| Current rent | Maximum increase | New rent |
|---|---|---|
| $1,500 | $75 | $1,575 |
| $1,800 | $90 | $1,890 |
| $2,255 *(HRM average, Aug 2026)* | $112.75 | $2,367.75 |
| $2,600 | $130 | $2,730 |

You may of course raise it by less. You cannot bank an unused increase and take 10% next year —
the cap resets, it does not accumulate.

## The four-month notice rule

Notice must be **in writing**, on the province's official notice-of-rent-increase form, and
delivered at least **four months** before the increase takes effect.

**Working backwards from common effective dates:**

| Increase effective | Serve notice by (approx.) |
|---|---|
| January 1, 2027 | September 1, 2026 |
| February 1, 2027 | October 1, 2026 |
| March 1, 2027 | November 1, 2026 |
| April 1, 2027 | December 1, 2026 |

Three things sink otherwise-valid notices:

- **Verbal or informal notice.** A text message saying "rent's going up in the new year" is not
  notice. Use the form.
- **Counting the months loosely.** Give yourself buffer. If you are within a week of the deadline,
  push the increase to the following month rather than risk it.
- **No proof of delivery.** If the tenant disputes it, you need to show *when* it was served.
  Keep a dated copy and a record of how it was delivered.

## When the 5% cap does not apply

This is the part that gets misread most often, in both directions.

**The cap follows the tenancy, not the unit.** It limits what you can charge *an existing tenant*.
Once a tenancy genuinely ends and a new tenant signs, there is no cap on what you may ask the new
tenant to pay — the market sets that number.

That distinction has become the most contested issue in Nova Scotia housing policy. Because
fixed-term leases end on a stated date rather than continuing automatically, some landlords have
used back-to-back fixed terms to reset rent above 5% between terms. The province has so far
declined to close this, and tenant advocates and opposition parties have pushed hard to change it.

**Our position, plainly:** using fixed-term leases as a routine device to engineer around the cap
is legally exposed and reputationally worse. Nova Scotia has been actively legislating in this area
and the rules can change. A turnover strategy built on a policy gap that may close is not a
strategy. Well-maintained units with tenants who stay, at lawful increases, outperform aggressive
churn once you price in vacancy, turnover cost, and the risk of a dispute at Residential Tenancies.

**Above-cap increases for major work** are handled through a separate process with its own notice
periods, evidentiary requirements, and tenant compensation — not by simply charging more. If you
are planning substantial renovations, get advice before you serve anything.

## What happens if you get it wrong

A tenant who believes an increase is invalid can dispute it through the **Residential Tenancies
Program**. If the notice was short, the increase over-cap, or the 12-month rule breached, the
increase can be struck down — and you may end up refunding the difference.

The practical cost is rarely the money. It is the months of delay, the paperwork, and a working
relationship with a paying tenant that is now adversarial.

## A simple annual process that works

1. **June–July** — review each unit against market rent and confirm the last increase date.
2. **Early August** — decide the increase per unit and prepare the notices.
3. **By September 1** — serve notice for a January 1 effective date. Record how and when.
4. **Log it** — diary the next eligible increase date immediately, so the 12-month clock is never
   guesswork.
5. **December** — remind the tenant before the new amount comes out, and update any pre-authorised
   payment.

The whole thing takes an afternoon a year per portfolio. Missing it costs you twelve months.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much can a landlord raise rent in Nova Scotia in 2027?**
Up to 5% in a 12-month period for an existing tenant. The cap is legislated through December 31, 2027.

**How much notice is required for a rent increase in Nova Scotia?**
Four months' written notice, on the official form, and no more than one increase per 12 months.

**When do I need to serve notice for a January 1, 2027 increase?**
By approximately September 1, 2026. Later than that and the increase cannot take effect January 1.

**Can I raise the rent more than 5%?**
Not for an existing tenant while the cap is in force. When a tenancy genuinely ends and a new
tenant signs, the cap does not limit the new rent. Above-cap increases tied to major renovations
run through a separate process with additional requirements.

**Does the rent cap apply to a new tenant?**
No. It restricts increases within an ongoing tenancy, not the rent agreed with a new tenant.

**When does the Nova Scotia rent cap end?**
It is currently set to run to December 31, 2027. It has been extended before, so treat that date as
provisional and watch for announcements.

**Can I raise the rent twice in one year?**
No. One increase per 12 months, measured from the previous increase.
