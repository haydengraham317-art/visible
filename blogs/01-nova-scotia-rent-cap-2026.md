# Nova Scotia Rent Cap 2026: How Much Can You Actually Raise Rent?

If you own a rental in Halifax, Bedford, Dartmouth or anywhere else in HRM, there are really only two
numbers that matter when you go to raise the rent: **5%**, and **four months**.

Get either one wrong and the increase is not just reduced — it can be thrown out entirely, leaving you
collecting last year's rent for another full year while your property tax, insurance and heating bills
carry on rising.

Here is exactly how the rent cap works right now, the deadline calendar most owners miss, and the four
errors we see most often.

---

## The short answer

**In 2026, a Nova Scotia landlord can raise an existing tenant's rent by a maximum of 5% per year, once
every 12 months, with at least four months' written notice on Form J.**

The 5% cap was extended by Bill 467 and now runs to **31 December 2027**.

---

## How much can a landlord raise rent in Nova Scotia?

Five percent. That is the hard ceiling for an existing tenant, and it applies regardless of when the
building was constructed — there is no new-build exemption in Nova Scotia the way there is in Ontario.

On a $1,800/month unit, 5% is **$90**. Your new rent is $1,890.

A few things owners routinely get wrong about what counts:

**The cap covers more than the rent line.** If your lease includes parking, electricity, heat, water or
storage and you start charging separately for any of them — or stop providing them — that is legally a
rent increase. It has to fit inside the same 5%. Pulling a $75/month parking spot out of an included
package on a $1,800 unit is a 4.2% increase before you have touched the rent itself.

**Once every 12 months, per tenant.** Not per calendar year. The clock runs from the last increase for
that specific tenancy, not from January.

**The cap does not apply to a brand-new tenant.** When a tenancy genuinely ends and you rent to someone
new, you set the price at market. That is the single biggest lever most Halifax owners have right now,
and given HRM's 2.7% vacancy rate and 5.4% year-over-year asking-rent growth, the gap between a
capped in-place rent and a market rent can be substantial.

---

## The four-month notice rule — and the deadline you are probably about to miss

Nova Scotia requires **four months' written notice** before a rent increase takes effect. That is the
longest notice period of any province in Canada, and it is where the majority of failed increases fail.

Notice must be delivered on the official **Form J — Notice of Rent Increase**. A text message, an email
saying "rent's going up in the new year," or a handwritten note taped to the door does not satisfy the
requirement, no matter how clearly it is worded or how well the tenant understood it.

| Increase takes effect | Form J must be served by |

| 1 January 2027 | **1 September 2026** |
| 1 February 2027 | 1 October 2026 |
| 1 March 2027 | 1 November 2026 |

| 1 May 2027 | 1 January 2027 |
| 1 June 2027 | 1 February 2027 |
| 1 July 2027 | 1 March 2027 |
| 1 August 2027 | 1 April 2027 |

> **If you want a rent increase effective 1 January 2027, your Form J has to be served by 1 September
> 2026.** For most HRM owners reading this in August, that deadline is days away — and if you are
> serving by mail rather than in person, build in extra days, because the clock runs from when notice is
> *properly served*, not when you dropped it in the postbox.
>
> Miss it, and the earliest you can raise that tenant's rent is 1 February 2027. On a $1,800 unit that
> single missed month is $90 you do not get back — and if the miss pushes you past the 12-month window,
> considerably more.

## The fixed-term lease question

This is the most contested issue in Nova Scotia rental housing, and if you own property in HRM you should
understand it clearly.

The rent cap applies to periodic leases — month-to-month and year-to-year — and it applies when a landlord
signs a **new fixed-term lease with the same tenant**.

But a fixed-term lease ends automatically on its stated end date. The landlord is under no legal
obligation to offer a renewal at all. And if a new tenancy is then formed, there is no restriction on the
starting rent.

Housing advocates call this the fixed-term lease loophole and have campaigned hard to have it closed.
As of mid-2026 the province has said it has **no plans to close it**, while acknowledging it is being
used in ways that push tenants out and push rents past the capped amount.

**Our position, plainly:** using consecutive fixed-term leases specifically to route around the rent cap
is legal today, and we still do not recommend building your strategy on it. It is politically unstable —
it could be legislated away with little warning — and it is corrosive to the thing that actually drives
returns on a rental, which is tenant retention.

Turnover is expensive. Vacancy, cleaning, painting, advertising, screening and a new lease typically
costs the equivalent of one to two months' rent. A good tenant who stays four years at a capped rent
almost always outperforms a churned unit that resets to market every eighteen months and sits empty for
three weeks each time. Run the numbers on your own property before you assume otherwise.

## Four mistakes that void a rent increase

**1. Wrong form, or no form.** It has to be Form J. This is the most common failure by a wide margin.

**2. Miscounting the four months.** Four months means four full months before the effective date, and it
runs from proper service. If there is any doubt about whether the tenant received it, you have a problem —
document how and when you served it, every time.

**3. Two increases inside 12 months.** Including the accidental version: a small increase in March, then a
"correction" in November. That second one is not enforceable.

**4. Forgetting that services count.** Removing something included in the lease, or charging separately
for it, is a rent increase. Add it to your percentage before you decide the increase is compliant.

If a tenant disputes an increase and the paperwork does not hold up, the increase can be voided outright.
You do not get a reduced increase — you get the old rent for another full year.

---

## What this means for your bottom line

The rent cap is not the real threat to a Halifax rental's performance. Missed deadlines and bad paperwork
are.

An owner who serves Form J correctly, on time, every 12 months compounds 5% annually. An owner who misses
a cycle here and there — because life got busy in August, or the form was wrong, or the notice could not
be proven served — loses ground permanently. Over five years on a $1,800 unit, that difference is
thousands of dollars in rent that was legally available and simply was not collected.

This is the least glamorous part of owning a rental and one of the most consequential.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much can a landlord raise rent in Nova Scotia in 2026?**
Up to 5% per year for an existing tenant, once every 12 months, with four months' written notice on
Form J. The 5% cap runs through 31 December 2027.

**How much notice does a landlord have to give for a rent increase in Nova Scotia?**
At least four months' written notice before the increase takes effect, served on Form J. For an increase
effective 1 January 2027, notice must be served by 1 September 2026.

**Can a landlord raise rent more than 5% in Nova Scotia?**
Not for an existing tenant while the cap is in force. The cap does not apply when you rent to a genuinely
new tenant, where you may set rent at market.

**Does the Nova Scotia rent cap apply to fixed-term leases?**
It applies when a landlord signs a new fixed-term lease with the same tenant. However, a fixed-term lease
ends automatically on its end date and the landlord is not required to renew — the widely discussed
"fixed-term lease loophole." The province has said it has no plans to close it as of mid-2026.

**When does the Nova Scotia rent cap end?**
It is currently scheduled to expire 31 December 2027, after being extended by Bill 467. It has been
extended more than once before.

**Is removing parking or electricity a rent increase?**
Yes. Charging separately for a service that was included in the lease, or withdrawing it, counts as a
rent increase and must fit within the 5% cap.

**Can a landlord raise rent twice in one year?**
No. Once every 12 months per tenancy, counted from the last increase for that tenant.

**What happens if my landlord didn't use Form J?**
The increase can be challenged and potentially voided entirely, in which case the previous rent continues.
